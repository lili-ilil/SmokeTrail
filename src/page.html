    SmokeTrail

    Follow the pollution from one cigarette: from the glowing coal into the smoker's lungs, out into the street, onto clothes and walls, down the drain, and into soil, plants and water.

    7,000+chemicals in tobacco smoke, at least 69 of them known carcinogens {{c:sg2010}}
    4.5 trillioncigarette butts discarded worldwide each year, a WHO estimate {{c:who2022}}
    1.6 million+people who do not smoke die each year from secondhand smoke {{c:who2026}}

    Stop 1 of 12Combustion

    Ignition: a small, hot reactor

    Lighting a cigarette starts burning and pyrolysis in a coal only a few millimetres long. During a puff, air is pulled through the coal and it reaches about 900 °C. Between puffs it keeps smouldering at a lower temperature {{c:sg2010}}.

    Two streams of smoke leave the cigarette. Mainstream smoke is pulled through the rod and filter into the smoker's mouth during a puff. Sidestream smoke rises from the smouldering coal into the air around it {{c:sg2010}}.

    {{chart:temp}}
    • 7,000+chemical compounds in tobacco smoke; at least 69 are known to cause cancer {{c:sg2010}}. A 2022 review lists 80 carcinogens in smoke {{c:lihecht2022}}.
    • 54%of the tobacco burned while the cigarette smouldered between puffs (550 s), against 46% during 20 s of puffing, in a classic machine test derived {{c:nrc1986}}
    • ≈11puffs per cigarette for real smokers (range 8–16), about 43 mL each {{c:zacny1996}}
    For experts: machines, temperatures and yields

    Reported temperatures differ with where and how they were measured. Between-puff values run from about 400 °C {{c:sg2010}} to 700–800 °C at the coal centre {{c:baker1974}}. Peak formation temperatures are about 900 °C for mainstream and 600 °C for sidestream smoke {{c:nrc1986}}.

    • ISO machine regime: 35 mL puff of 2 s every 60 s, filter ventilation open. Health Canada Intense: 55 mL every 30 s, vents blocked {{c:iarc83,hammond2007}}.
    • EU ceilings measured with the ISO regime: 10 mg tar, 1 mg nicotine and 10 mg CO per cigarette {{c:eu2014}}. People puff harder than the machine, so these numbers understate what many smokers take in {{c:hammond2007}}.
    • 16 constituents of smoke are IARC Group 1 carcinogens, among them benzene, formaldehyde, benzo[a]pyrene, NNK, NNN, cadmium and polonium-210 {{c:iarc100e}}.

    Stop 2 of 12The smoker

    Inhalation: the lungs as a filter

    Mainstream smoke is a dense aerosol of sub-micrometre droplets in a hot gas. Its particles have a mass median diameter of about 0.35–0.40 µm {{c:iarc83}}, small enough to travel deep into the lungs. Most of what is inhaled stays in the body.

    {{chart:retention}}

    What the lungs do not keep is breathed out again: by these figures, roughly 20–40% of the particles derived. That exhaled smoke is the start of the next stop.

    For experts: deposition and its uncertainty
    • Retention depends on puff volume, depth of inhalation and breath-holding. Older studies report particle retention of 90% or more, and CO retention that rarely exceeds 50% {{c:nrc1986}}.
    • Lung deposition modelling (MPPD) gives a total deposited fraction of 0.61 for mainstream and 0.47 for exhaled smoke particles {{c:sahu2013}}.
    • In mainstream smoke, nicotine is mostly in the particle phase {{c:nrc1986}}.

    Stop 3 of 12Into the air

    Out into the air: two plumes become one

    Each breath out releases what the lungs did not keep. The exhaled particles have grown to about 1.5 times their inhaled size {{c:sahu2013}}. Meanwhile the coal keeps smouldering. Sidestream smoke forms at a lower temperature with less oxygen, so for many toxicants a cigarette releases more into the air than the smoker inhales {{c:nrc1986}}.

    {{chart:ratio}}

    Secondhand smoke is the mixture of the two. Sidestream smoke supplies nearly all of its gases and over half of its particles; exhaled mainstream smoke supplies about 15–43% of the particle mass {{c:epa1992}}.

    For experts: ageing of the aerosol
    • Fresh sidestream particles have a geometric mean diameter near 0.1 µm {{c:iarc83}}. Within seconds coagulation cuts the particle number and the particles grow.
    • On dilution about a quarter of the particle mass evaporates {{c:bakerproctor1990}}. In aged secondhand smoke about 95% of the nicotine is in the gas phase {{c:epa1992}}.
    • The ratios above fall when the machine puffs harder; for NNK and NNN they can drop below 1 {{c:iarc83}}.
    • The common claim that 85% of the smoke in a room is sidestream smoke traces back to a 1986 statement without primary data {{c:ota1986}}.

    Stop 4 of 12Dispersion

    Wind: dilution, not disappearance

    Outdoors the plume is carried downwind and mixed with cleaner air. Concentrations drop steeply over the first few metres, but right next to a smoker they can be very high: within 0.5 m, peaks exceeded 1,000 µg/m³ and averages over one cigarette exceeded 200 µg/m³ {{c:klepeis2007}}.

    {{chart:distance}}
    • 188 vs 21µg/m³ PM2.5 (geometric means) in 1,238 venues where people smoked and 584 smoke-free venues, in 32 countries {{c:hyland2008}}
    • 31 vs 3µg/m³ median PM2.5 in 93 homes where someone smoked indoors and 17 smoke-free homes {{c:semple2015}}
    For experts: chemistry in the air
    • Gas-phase nicotine reacts with OH radicals (k = 8.4 × 10⁻¹¹ cm³ molecule⁻¹ s⁻¹), a lifetime of about 6.6 h indoors {{c:borduas2016}} and about 1–2 h outdoors in daylight {{c:aquilina2021}}.
    • Fine particles last much longer. An observed e-folding lifetime of accumulation-mode aerosol was about 14 days; models give 5–27 days {{c:kristiansen2016}}.
    • Outdoor air in cities contains about 0.4–82 ng/m³ nicotine. With a new marker, tobacco smoke made up 0.03–0.08% of outdoor PM in three cities {{c:aquilina2021}}; a 1994 Los Angeles study estimated 1.0–1.3% of fine-particle mass {{c:rogge1994}}.
    • For a single cigarette at low wind (0.41 m/s), levels approached background beyond about 2 m {{c:klepeis2007}}.

    Stop 5 of 12Deposition

    Rain: washing the sky, unevenly

    Smoke leaves the air by deposition. It settles or sticks to surfaces (dry deposition), or cloud droplets and raindrops capture it (wet deposition). Raindrops catch large particles and very small ones well. Particles of 0.1–1 µm, the size of aged tobacco smoke, are the hardest to catch. This dip in efficiency is known as the Greenfield gap {{c:wang2010}}.

    {{chart:scav}}
    • ≈28 hof steady rain would be needed to remove 63% of 0.1–1 µm particles at the observed coefficient of 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ derived
    • >99%of dissolved nicotine is protonated in clean rain (pH about 5.6), from its pKa of 7.89 {{c:clayton2013}} derived. Nicotine is fully miscible with water, so gas-phase nicotine dissolves readily into drops {{c:bpdb}}.

    Gap in the evidence: we found no study that measured nicotine in rainwater itself.

    For experts: scavenging and solubility
    • Removal of 1 µm particles by 5 mm of rain ranges from 0.1% to 30.5% depending on the parameterisation {{c:wang2010}}.
    • Henry's law solubility of nicotine: 3.3 × 10³ mol m⁻³ Pa⁻¹ (an estimate) {{c:sander2023}}. A pesticide database lists 9.0 × 10⁻⁴ Pa m³ mol⁻¹ {{c:bpdb}}. The two estimates differ by about a factor of 3.
    • In Berlin, waters receiving stormwater carried about twice as much nicotine, and canals up to 16 times more after heavy rain. The main source was butts washed off streets rather than rain itself {{c:venohr2025}}.

    Stop 6 of 12People as carriers

    Clothes, hair and skin: smoke that walks away

    Smoke compounds stick to people. Nicotine and other semi-volatile compounds sorb to hair, skin and fabric and are carried into places where nobody smoked. In a non-smoking cinema, audiences brought in 35 tobacco-related compounds on their bodies and clothes; one screening exposed the room to the equivalent of 1–10 cigarettes of secondhand smoke {{c:sheu2020}}.

    {{chart:dermal}}
    • 660 ngnicotine per finger wipe on smokers, against 0.47 ng on non-smokers {{c:matt2011}}
    • 40 minafter washing with soap, nicotine on smokers' fingers was back near its starting level {{c:northrup2022}}
    • 19 monthslater, fabrics still held thirdhand smoke chemicals; cotton released about 41 times more nicotine than polyester {{c:bahl2014}}
    For experts: skin as a reservoir
    • Hair grows about 1 cm per month, so segments of hair record nicotine exposure month by month {{c:aldelaimy2002}}.
    • After dermal exposure, nicotine biomarkers kept being excreted for the full 84 h of follow-up, which points to a reservoir in the skin {{c:beko2018}}.

    Stop 7 of 12Indoors

    Thirdhand smoke: the room remembers

    Thirdhand smoke is the residue that stays after the cigarette is out {{c:winickoff2009}}. In a furnished room, 95% to over 99% of the nicotine ended up on surfaces rather than in the air at equilibrium {{c:singer2004}}. Walls, carpets, curtains, furniture and dust become reservoirs that release nicotine back into the air {{c:singer2002,singer2003}}.

    nicotine (on surfaces) + HONO → NNA + NNK

    Nitrous acid, a common indoor pollutant, turns sorbed nicotine into tobacco-specific nitrosamines. Yield: 6.7–9.1% of the reacted nicotine by mass, about 7 parts NNA to 1 part NNK {{c:sleiman2010}}. NNK is an IARC Group 1 carcinogen {{c:iarc100e}}.

    nicotine + O₃ → ultrafine particles, formaldehyde

    Ozone reacting with nicotine forms secondary organic aerosol, 4–9% by mass {{c:sleiman2010b}}, and on surfaces yields formaldehyde and other products {{c:destaillats2006}}.

    1. 3 daysAlmost all sorbed nicotine was still on the surfaces of a test room {{c:singer2002}}.
    2. 62 daysHomes left empty for a median of 62 days and cleaned still had more surface nicotine than former non-smoker homes (10.0 vs 1.5 µg/m²), and the new residents had more nicotine on their fingers {{c:matt2011}}.
    3. 3 monthsAfter a casino went smoke-free, it took about 3 months for nicotine and nitrosamines in dust to fall by 90% {{c:matt2018}}.
    4. 19 monthsFabrics still held thirdhand smoke chemicals {{c:bahl2014}}.
    • 0.05–0.25 gof dust swallowed by an infant each day, more than twice the adult rate {{c:sleiman2010}}
    • 77%of smokers' homes (and 64% of non-smokers' homes) had settled dust in which nitrosamine cancer risk for children aged 1–6 exceeded the US EPA upper-bound risk {{c:ramirez2014}}
    • 2.4×the odds of malignant lymphoma in cats from smoking households, in a case–control study {{c:bertone2002}}
    For experts: why airing out is not enough
    • Indirect routes (smoke still in the air plus re-emission from surfaces) accounted for about half of potential nicotine exposure during non-smoking periods, at all ventilation rates tested {{c:singer2003}}.
    • Homes of parents who smoked only outdoors had 5–7 times the contamination of non-smoker homes; smoking indoors added another 3–8 times {{c:matt2004}}.
    • Thirdhand smoke extracts and NNA caused DNA strand breaks in human cells; NNA is not present in fresh secondhand smoke {{c:hang2013}}.

    Stop 8 of 12Litter

    The butt: a filter full of what it caught

    A cigarette filter is a plug of about 12,000–15,000 cellulose acetate fibres {{c:novotny2009,green2023}}: a plastic. After smoking it holds part of what it trapped. About 6 trillion cigarettes are made each year {{c:zafeiridou2018}} and WHO estimates that 4.5 trillion butts are discarded {{c:who2022}}. In studies, 65–80% of smokers dropped their butt on the ground {{c:who2022,novotny2014,nitschke2023}}.

    • ≈2 mgnicotine left in a butt; about half can wash out in the first 30 minutes of rain {{c:igb2025,rodergreen2014}}
    • ≈1 butt/LLeachate at this strength killed half of the test fish within 96 hours {{c:slaughter2011}}
    • 11 of 12metals tested (including Pb, Cu, Zn, Ni, Cr) were found in water within a day of soaking smoked butts {{c:moerman2011}}
    • 7.5–14 yearsmodelled time for plastic filters to lose 99% of their mass in compost and on the soil surface {{c:joly2018}}
    For experts: fibres, PAHs and thresholds
    • Littered butts may release about 0.3 million tonnes of microfibres a year, comparable to household laundry {{c:green2023}}.
    • Leachate from butts contained 3.0–5.7 µg/L of PAHs between 4 hours and 21 days {{c:dobaradaran2020}}.
    • The Swiss ecotoxicology centre estimates that one butt can push about 1,000 L of water above the nicotine no-effect level of 2.4 µg/L {{c:oekotox2019}}.
    • In a field study, filters lost about 38% of their mass in two years {{c:bonanomi2015}}.
    • Butts and filters were the most common item on European beaches in 2014–2016, 16.7% of all items counted {{c:hanke2016}}.

    Stop 9 of 12Soil and plants

    Soil and plants: nicotine in the herb bed

    A butt in soil keeps leaching. Nicotine is very water soluble and only moderately held by soil (estimated Koc ≈ 100 mL/g {{c:bpdb}}), so it can move down with water. Soil microbes also break it down quickly; one model gives a half-life of about half a day in soil {{c:seckar2008}}. Plants take it up through their roots.

    • >20×the EU maximum residue level: nicotine found in peppermint and basil grown with only 1 butt per m² of soil {{c:selmar2018}}
    • 0.01 mg/kgdefault EU maximum residue level for nicotine in food; some herbs, spices and teas have temporary higher limits {{c:eu2023mrl}}
    • >6 mg/kgnicotine in fresh peppermint leaves exposed to the smoke of 11 cigarettes, so leaves take it up from the air as well {{c:selmar2015}}
    {{chart:plants}}
    For experts: data quality
    • The soil sorption coefficient and the 2-day soil half-life in the pesticide database are estimates, not measurements {{c:bpdb}}. We found no measured Koc in the peer-reviewed literature.
    • The "readily biodegradable" assessment comes from authors affiliated with the tobacco industry {{c:seckar2008}}.
    • The percentages in the growth chart come from press coverage; the paper's abstract reports significant reductions in germination and shoot length {{c:green2019}}.

    Stop 10 of 12Water

    Water: from the drain to the lake and the aquifer

    Rain carries butts and their leachate into storm drains; in one city survey, butts were 31% of the items found in drains {{c:kozikowski2024}}. In separate sewer systems, stormwater flows straight to streams and lakes. Nicotine also reaches water through wastewater, as cotinine and other metabolites in smokers' urine.

    {{chart:water}}
    • 90–99%of cotinine removed in Swiss wastewater treatment plants; N-formylnornicotine was not removed {{c:buerge2008}}
    • 16×higher nicotine in Berlin canals after heavy rain, mainly from washed-out butts {{c:venohr2025}}
    • GroundwaterNicotine has been found in aquifers beneath septic systems {{c:godfrey2007}}. We found no Swiss groundwater data on nicotine.
    For experts: thresholds disagree
    • The no-effect level used here (2.4 µg/L) is an EU value cited by the Swiss ecotoxicology centre {{c:oekotox2019}}. The Berlin team names 100 ng/L for water fleas {{c:igb2025}}.
    • Small streams with a large share of treated wastewater are expected to reach a few hundred ng/L of nicotine metabolites {{c:buerge2008}}.

    Stop 11 of 12Health

    Bystanders: there is no safe level

    The US Surgeon General concluded in 2006 that there is no risk-free level of exposure to secondhand smoke {{c:sg2006}}. IARC classifies involuntary smoking as carcinogenic to humans, Group 1 {{c:iarc83}}.

    {{chart:risk}}
    • ChildrenSecondhand smoke causes sudden infant death syndrome, middle-ear disease, lower respiratory illness and asthma {{c:sg2006}}.
    • 1.6 million+deaths a year among people who do not smoke (GBD 2023) {{c:who2026}}. For 2004 the estimate was 603,000, 28% of them children {{c:oberg2011}}.
    • −8.2%respiratory mortality after smoking bans in Swiss cantons {{c:vicedo2016b}}. In Graubünden, heart attacks fell 21% in the two years after its ban {{c:bonetti2011}}.
    For experts: why the global numbers changed

    Estimates of deaths from secondhand smoke rose from 603,000 (2004 data) {{c:oberg2011}} to more than 1.6 million (GBD 2023) {{c:who2026}}. The increase reflects new data and methods as well as population change, so the figures should not be read as a trend.

    Early-term births fell 5.0% after Swiss smoking bans {{c:vicedo2016}}.

    How to read these numbers

    Where the numbers come from

    • Every figure is taken from the source cited next to it. Ranges show the spread across brands, studies or methods.
    • Values marked derived were calculated on this site from cited figures, for example the share burned between puffs or the hours of rain needed to remove fine particles.
    • Emission factors come from smoking machines and toxicity values from laboratory tests. Real exposure depends on ventilation, wind, distance and dilution.
    • The landscape is a drawing, not a model. Distances in the wind stop follow the cited study; everything else is schematic.

    Where sources disagree

    • Global deaths from secondhand smoke: 603,000 (2004 data), about 1.3 million (earlier WHO estimate) and more than 1.6 million (GBD 2023).
    • Chemicals in smoke: about 5,300 to more than 7,000; carcinogens: 69 to 80.
    • Coal temperature between puffs: about 400 °C to 800 °C.
    • Share of smokers who litter their butt: 65% to 80%.
    • Nicotine threshold for aquatic life: 2.4 µg/L or 100 ng/L.

    Gaps we could not fill

    • No study measuring nicotine in rainwater.
    • No measured count of butts littered in Switzerland, and no Swiss groundwater data on nicotine.
    • No measured soil sorption coefficient for nicotine.
    • No primary data behind the often-quoted "85% sidestream" share of room smoke.
